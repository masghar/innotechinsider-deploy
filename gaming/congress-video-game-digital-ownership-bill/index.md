# Congress Introduces Landmark Bill to End the Illusion of Game Ownership

> A new bipartisan bill in Congress wants to force digital storefronts to grant real ownership rights—or admit you're merely renting your library indefinitely.

By InnotechInsider Staff · Gaming · Oct 19, 2026
Canonical URL: https://innotechinsider.com/gaming/congress-video-game-digital-ownership-bill/

> **TL;DR** *A bipartisan federal bill introduced this week would force digital storefronts to stop using the word "buy" for revocable software licenses, mandate offline fallback patches when online servers shut down, and grant broad preservation rights to players.*

For two decades, the video game industry has operated on a convenient legal fiction. You click a bright green button marked "Buy Now," hand over $70, and watch a digital title download to your drive. But as millions of players have discovered the hard way, you didn't buy a game. You bought a conditional, revocable, and increasingly fragile license to access someone else’s software until an executive decides running the authentication server is no longer accretive to shareholder value.

That fiction may finally be running out of runway.

On Capitol Hill this week, a bipartisan coalition introduced the **Digital Interactive Entertainment Preservation and Ownership (DIEPO) Act**, the most aggressive federal attempt yet to dismantle the industry's smoke-and-mirrors licensing architecture. If passed, the bill will radically reorder how storefronts like Steam, the PlayStation Network, Xbox Live, and the Epic Games Store sell software to American consumers—imposing heavy fines on companies that market revocable licenses as sales, and legally requiring publishers to provide offline play capabilities or server binaries when they shut down a game.

The move marks a turning point in digital consumer rights, elevating a grievance long dismissed as niche enthusiast whining into a mainstream regulatory reckoning.

## The Breaking Point: From Disappearing Licenses to Outright Brickings

The legislative push didn't happen in a vacuum. It is the culmination of years of escalating consumer hostility toward publisher practices.

When Ubisoft pulled the plug on *The Crew* in 2024 and revoked the game license from players' digital libraries altogether, it sparked an international backlash that organized under grassroots efforts like the [Stop Killing Games](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stop_Killing_Games) initiative. While California moved quickly to pass AB 2426 in late 2024—barring digital storefronts from using terms like "buy" or "purchase" unless customers receive permanent offline access—state-level measures merely forced platforms to add passive-aggressive disclaimers at checkout.

Consumers didn't want warning labels telling them they were getting fleeced; they wanted property rights.

"Consumers understand the difference between subscribing to Netflix and buying a movie on Blu-ray," said Representative Marcus Vance (D-WA), who co-introduced the bill alongside Representative Elena Alvarez (R-TX). "Yet the gaming industry has spent fifteen years deliberately blurring that line. When you pay retail price for a piece of interactive art, you shouldn't have to worry that the publisher can remotely reach onto your hard drive two years later and delete it."

The debate strikes at the heart of modern software distribution. As the industry migrates deeper into cloud services and hybrid deployment models, as covered in our ongoing coverage of [biz it](/biz-it/) infrastructure, the physical detachment between software and consumer has made arbitrary product obsolescence the corporate default.

## The Three Pillars of the DIEPO Act

The bill does not simply demand truth in advertising; it creates affirmative obligations for publishers, storefronts, and platform holders. It is structured around three core regulatory mandates:

### 1. Mandatory End-of-Life (EOL) Contingency Plans
Before a commercial video game can be sold digitally to U.S. consumers, publishers must file a designated sunset strategy. If a game features single-player content or peer-to-peer multiplayer capabilities tied to an online check-in server, the publisher must release a patch upon server termination that removes server dependencies or unlocks dedicated community hosting.

### 2. The Death of the Deceptive "Buy" Button
Mirroring and expanding California’s initial efforts, storefronts will face Federal Trade Commission (FTC) enforcement under [Section 5 of the FTC Act](https://www.ftc.gov/legal-library/browse/statutes/federal-trade-commission-act) for deceptive practices if they display "Buy," "Purchase," or "Own" without explicitly clarifying the nature of the transaction. If the consumer cannot export an unrestricted, un-DRM'd copy of the software or run it perpetually without an internet connection, the UI must read "License" or "Rent."

### 3. Broad Community Preservation Protections
The legislation carve-outs robust exemptions under the [Digital Millennium Copyright Act](https://www.copyright.gov/dmca/) (DMCA) for archivists, museums, and hobbyists. It legalizes the reverse-engineering and distribution of server emulation code for software that has been commercially abandoned, insulating community preservationists from civil copyright and anti-circumvention lawsuits.

| Provision | Current Standard Practice | Under the DIEPO Act |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Storefront Terminology** | "Buy," "Purchase," or "Own" used universally | Must use "License" or "Lease" unless fully un-DRM'd |
| **Server Shutdowns** | Games become unplayable paperweights | Publisher must release offline patch or server binaries |
| **Community Emulation** | Targets of DMCA takedowns and cease-and-desists | Federally protected non-profit reverse-engineering |
| **Single-Player Tethering** | Always-online DRM can render campaigns inaccessible | Offline mode mandatory once verification servers close |
| **Regulatory Enforcement** | Patchwork state consumer laws; minimal oversight | FTC enforcement; up to $50,000 per violation |

## The Industry Mobilizes: "You Cannot Patch the Impossible"

Unsurprisingly, the Entertainment Software Association (ESA)—the primary lobbying arm for major publishers—has reacted to the bill with furious resistance. Within hours of the bill’s introduction, the organization released a statement warning that the legislation would "stifle technological innovation and impose unworkable technical burdens on American game studios."

The industry's technical counterargument is not entirely without merit. Over the last decade, games have evolved from self-contained executables into distributed cloud architectures. In many modern live-service titles, significant portions of game logic, physics processing, and matchmaking do not run on the local client machine; they run across sprawling microservices hosted on AWS or Azure.

Publishers argue that requiring a studio to "patch out" server dependencies is not as simple as flipping an offline switch. In many cases, turning a live-service game into a standalone offline product would require months of re-engineering, rewriting proprietary backends, and potentially open-sourcing licensed third-party code that studios do not have the legal right to distribute.

There are also growing concerns around software security and intellectual property exposure. When publishers are forced to release server-side code or strip protective wrappers, enterprise cybersecurity teams worry about vulnerability disclosures and asset extraction, themes that regularly intersect with broader debates in [future tech](/future-tech/) policy.

"If Congress forces small and mid-sized studios to guarantee twenty years of perpetual functionality for every experimental multiplayer game they ship, those studios simply won't ship those games," argued an ESA policy director who spoke on background. "It will kill risk-taking and consolidate the market even further into the hands of mega-publishers who can afford to absorb regulatory penalties."

## The Counter-Argument: Publishers Engineered Their Own Problem

Preservationists and consumer advocates reject the industry's hand-wringing out of hand. They point out that publishers intentionally engineered these architectural dependencies precisely to prevent unauthorized preservation, crack down on piracy, and compel players to migrate to newer sequels.

For decades, PC gaming thrived on user-run dedicated servers. Titles like *Half-Life*, *Unreal Tournament*, and *Quake* did not require publisher intervention to survive decades past their commercial prime; players spun up their own hosting hardware, configured their own firewalls, and kept digital worlds alive through community stewardship. The industry deliberately dismantled that model in the late 2000s in favor of centralized matchmaking and walled-garden authentication.

"The industry wants to have it both ways," says Ross Scott, the consumer advocate whose grassroot campaigning helped push federal lawmakers toward action. "They want the legal immunity of treating games like ephemeral broadcast services, but they want the revenues and marketing power of treating games like durable physical goods. You cannot sell a product for $70, charge for virtual items inside of it, and then claim you have the right to erase it from existence without a backward glance."

## What Comes Next

The DIEPO Act enters Congress with surprising tailwinds. Unlike heavily partisan regulatory fights over social media algorithms or AI platform liability, digital ownership has become an unexpected locus of cross-party alignment.

Conservative lawmakers view the bill through the lens of private property rights and pushback against corporate control over what individuals run on hardware they own. Progressive lawmakers view it as a critical consumer protection measure against monopolistic platform lock-in and anti-competitive licensing tactics.

The bill faces an arduous road through committee hearings, where the ESA and its corporate backers will undoubtedly try to water down the end-of-life mandates with loopholes for games that rely on third-party licensed technology or "economically unfeasible" architectural conversions.

Yet the mere introduction of the DIEPO Act signals that the era of passive digital serfdom is ending. For twenty years, technology companies have treated physical ownership as a quaint relic of an analog past, confident that consumer convenience would always outweigh digital autonomy. By dragging the question of game ownership into the federal spotlight, Congress is forcing the industry to answer a question it has spent two decades trying to dodge: If buying isn't owning, why should anyone pay you at all?
