# Anker's Modular 13-in-1 Docking Station Drops $50 Ahead of Black Friday

> Anker has discounted its modular 13-in-1 desktop docking station by $50, pairing full workstation connectivity with a pop-out USB-C travel hub for mobile workflows.

Canonical URL: https://innotechinsider.com/biz-it/anker-modular-13-in-1-dock-deal-travel-hub/

> **TL;DR** Anker has trimmed $50 off its hybrid 13-in-1 docking station, bringing a versatile workstation hub with a detachable, travel-ready USB-C puck down to one of its lowest prices of the season.

Desk clutter remains an annoying constant for hybrid workers split between remote offices and daily commutes. Docking stations solve the cable problem at home, but hitting the road typically means carrying a separate dongle or swapping cords out of a stationary hub. Anker attempted to bridge that exact friction with its 13-in-1 docking station, built around a central nesting design: a full-sized desktop base that houses a pop-out, 5-port travel hub.

Ahead of the holiday shopping rush, public listings on Amazon and Anker’s direct storefront show the dock discounted by $50, dropping its retail price from $199 down to $149. For professionals retooling their [biz it](/biz-it/) hardware setups before year-end budget cycles close, it offers an atypical two-in-one proposition.

## The Modular Mechanics

Rather than forcing users to purchase both a heavy desktop dock and a separate mobile adapter, the unit splits its functions physically. When docked together, the complete unit functions as a conventional 13-in-1 desktop command center. It routes host power, drives multiple external displays via [DisplayPort Alternate Mode standard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/DisplayPort) implementations, and handles wired Gigabit Ethernet alongside legacy USB-A peripherals.

Pushing the release mechanism ejects the travel module—a compact puck that fits into a pocket. The puck retains an independent USB-C host cord, two USB-A ports, an SD card reader, and an HDMI port supporting 4K video at 60Hz.

The stationary base does not go entirely dark when the puck is removed, though its high-speed pass-through capabilities are naturally curtailed until the travel hub is slotted back into its proprietary interconnect bay.

## Thermal Management and Power Constraints

One common issue with nested consumer electronics is heat buildup, especially when routing power under load. The dock handles standard [USB Power Delivery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/USB_hardware#USB_Power_Delivery) protocols, pulling from an external 130W DC power brick to supply 100W clean to the host machine while reserving 30W for connected drives and accessories.

Because the travel puck relies on passive aluminum cooling, it warms noticeably during sustained 4K projection or continuous high-throughput file transfers from external NVMe drives. When seated inside the base station, however, the dock’s secondary chassis acts as an auxiliary heat sink, preventing the thermal throttling that occasionally plagues cheaper plastic dongles.

For [apple](/apple/) hardware users, it is worth noting macOS display limitations: like most non-Thunderbolt hubs relying on Multi-Stream Transport (MST), Macs connected to this dock can only mirror secondary external displays rather than extending them across two independent monitors. Windows machines, operating under native MST support certified by [USB-IF specifications](https://www.usb.org), can drive two discrete external screens without issue.

## Value Analysis

At its standard $200 price point, Anker’s hybrid dock carried a steep novelty premium compared to buying a solid budget dock and an independent $25 travel hub. At $149, the math looks significantly more practical.

You are paying for the physical convenience of having your desk cables remain routed, neatly tucked behind your monitors, while pulling out the exact ports you need for a field presentation or coffee-shop work session. Users running dual high-refresh-rate gaming panels or seeking 40Gbps Thunderbolt 4 bandwidth will still need to look elsewhere. But for typical office workloads, dual-monitor productivity, and hot-desking, this discount makes a genuinely functional design far easier to recommend.
