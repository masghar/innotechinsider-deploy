# Nvidia Director Mark Stevens Sold Over $1B in Stock Without a 10b5-1 Plan

> SEC disclosures reveal Nvidia board member Mark Stevens executed massive open-market stock disposals without relying on a pre-scheduled Rule 10b5-1 trading plan.

By InnotechInsider Staff · Business & IT · Nov 15, 2026
Canonical URL: https://innotechinsider.com/biz-it/nvidia-director-mark-stevens-insider-stock-sale-10b5-1/

> **TL;DR** Regulatory filings show longtime Nvidia board director Mark Stevens offloaded 4.2 million shares—surpassing $1 billion in proceeds—through discretionary transactions rather than pre-scheduled Rule 10b5-1 trading programs, intensifying scrutiny over corporate governance inside semiconductor heavyweights.

Regulatory disclosures filed with the [U.S. Securities and Exchange Commission](https://www.sec.gov) confirm that longtime Nvidia board member Mark Stevens sold roughly 4.2 million shares across recent quarters, generating over $1 billion in cash proceeds.

What has caught the attention of compliance officers and financial analysts is the mechanism of the sales: Stevens executed the disposals directly on the open market, bypassing the standard protection of a pre-established [Rule 10b5-1 trading plan](https://www.sec.gov/rules/final/2022/33-11138.pdf).

The timing has magnified concerns following mounting public controversies and executive scrutiny around chief executive Jensen Huang. As enterprise spending continues to ripple through [biz it](/biz-it/) architectures worldwide, institutional investors are placing board oversight under stricter microscopes.

## The Mechanics of Discretionary Insider Sales

Rule 10b5-1 plans were created by the SEC to shield corporate insiders from allegations of trading on material non-public information (MNPI). When executives or directors establish these plans, they lock in transaction dates, share volumes, and price floors well in advance, accompanied by mandatory cooling-off periods instituted during recent regulatory overhauls.

Filings reveal that Stevens, who previously served on [Nvidia's board of directors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mark_Stevens_(venture_capitalist)) during the company's early years before returning as a director in 2008, opted instead for discretionary transactions. While discretionary selling is entirely legal provided the insider does not possess material non-public information at the moment the trade is executed, it leaves the seller without the affirmative defense that an automated program provides.

| Trading Parameter | Discretionary Open-Market Sale | Rule 10b5-1 Trading Program |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Execution Trigger** | Real-time broker order by insider | Pre-determined formula, date, or price |
| **Cooling-Off Period** | None required | Mandatory 90–120 days for directors |
| **Legal Affirmative Defense**| No | Yes (under Exchange Act Rule 10b5-1) |
| **Form 4 Disclosure** | Must disclose trade within 2 business days | Discloses trade and checkmarks Rule 10b5-1 box |

Discretionary trades of this magnitude are rare among top-tier Silicon Valley companies, where corporate counsels typically push board members toward automated liquidation schedules to avoid market turbulence and reputational exposure.

## Governance Questions Compound Under Broader Scrutiny

The multi-million-share exit coincided with an increasingly turbulent stretch for Nvidia's executive leadership. Public reporting over recent months has centered on internal friction, scrutiny of executive relationships, and heightened federal attention directed at the hardware giant's software ecosystem lock-in.

Large-scale insider transactions can trigger significant market sensitivities, particularly in high-beta segments driven by enterprise adoption of [ai](/ai/) computing clusters. When an insider chooses to execute spot sales totaling hundreds of millions of dollars at a clip without an algorithmic buffer, market makers parse the decision for hidden signals.

Neither Stevens nor Nvidia responded to inquiries regarding the decision to execute the transactions outside a 10b5-1 safe harbor.

## Heightened Regulatory Attention on Tech Insiders

The timing comes as the SEC continues strict enforcement of modernized disclosure standards for executive trading. Regulators have consistently warned corporate boards that discretionary selling shortly before public disclosures—or during periods marked by internal turbulence—invites investigative scrutiny.

Corporate governance experts note that outside directors are expected to act as checks on executive leadership, not expose the company to headline risk through unshielded liquidations. With Stevens remaining one of Nvidia's largest individual shareholders alongside Huang, fund managers are watching closely to see whether the board implements stricter internal trading mandates or continues to permit discretionary multi-million-share exits.
