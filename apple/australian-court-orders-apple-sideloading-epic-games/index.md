# Australian Court Orders Apple to Open iOS to Rival App Stores

> Australia's Federal Court has ruled against Apple in Epic Games' antitrust suit. The tech giant must permit third-party app stores and billing on iPhones.

By InnotechInsider Staff · Apple · Oct 22, 2026
Canonical URL: https://innotechinsider.com/apple/australian-court-orders-apple-sideloading-epic-games/

> **TL;DR** Australia’s Federal Court has ruled that Apple violated national competition laws, ordering the company to permit alternative app stores and outside payment engines on iPhones across the country.

SYDNEY — Apple’s fortress around the iPhone has suffered another major breach. In a decision handed down this morning, the [Federal Court of Australia](https://www.fedcourt.gov.au) ruled that Apple breached section 46 of the Competition and Consumer Act 2010 by leveraging its operating system exclusivity to stifle distribution and payments competition.

The ruling marks the conclusion of a legal offensive launched by Epic Games in late 2020. Justice Jonathan Beach rejected Apple’s long-standing defense that the App Store constitutes a single, indivisible product with iOS security, finding instead that Cupertino effectively runs an independent software distribution market where it engaged in anti-competitive conduct.

Under the terms of the pending injunction, Apple has four months to give Australian users access to third-party marketplaces and allow developers to steer customers toward external web payments without punitive platform surcharges.

## How the Global Mandates Compare

Australia’s verdict mirrors actions taken by the European Commission under the Digital Markets Act, but goes further by explicitly restricting Apple from levying compensatory runtime fees on alternative marketplaces.

| Market | Legal Basis | Third-Party Stores Permitted? | Core Technology Fee / Surcharge | Direct Sideloading (Web)? |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **European Union** | Digital Markets Act (2024) | Yes | Yes (€0.50 per install over 1M) | Yes (authorized notarization) |
| **United States** | *Epic v. Apple* Rule 65 Injunction | No | Yes (up to 27% outside link fee) | No |
| **Australia** | Competition & Consumer Act (2026) | Yes | Prohibited pending review | Pending technical remedy |

The judgment is an outright validation of the regulatory posture long maintained by the [Australian Competition and Consumer Commission](https://www.accc.gov.au), which intervened during early hearings to argue that closed mobile ecosystems artificially inflate consumer software prices.

## A Stricter Constraint on Cupertino’s Re-Monetization Playbook

When Apple adapted to European regulations two years ago, it introduced a 50-cent Core Technology Fee (CTF) for installs beyond one million downloads. That tactic drew fierce blowback from developers who claimed the levy wiped out margins for freemium utilities and indie games.

Justice Beach’s findings preemptively blunt that maneuver. The judgment notes that replacement fee structures designed primarily to offset the elimination of the 30 percent App Store commission violate the spirit of the court's order. While Apple retains the authority to implement reasonable notarization checks to guard against malicious software, it cannot tie those security checks to proprietary billing collection.

For developers in the [gaming](/gaming/) sector, the outcome provides immediate strategic relief. Epic confirmed it will bring its Epic Games Store and *Fortnite* natively to Australian iOS devices as soon as the compliance window opens.

## Apple Prepares Appeal as Global Pressure Mounts

Apple signaled it will immediately seek a stay and petition the Full Court of the Federal Court.

"Today's decision disregards the rigorous protections we have built into iOS to keep Australian consumers safe from malware, fraud, and invasive tracking," an Apple spokesperson said in an emailed statement. "We intend to challenge this ruling to protect our users’ privacy and the intellectual property rights that allow our ecosystem to thrive."

The legal mechanics now shift toward technical remedies. Similar to previous disputes cataloged on [Wikipedia's Epic Games v. Apple timeline](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Epic_Games_v._Apple), the devil will remain in the implementation. If the court rejects Apple's request for a stay during the appellate process, Australian engineers will see foreign marketplaces arrive on domestic iPhones before the end of the fiscal year.

Enterprise software vendors and mobile studios operating across [biz it](/biz-it/) channels are already auditing their checkout architectures. After years of watching fragmented court battles in San Jose, Brussels, and Tokyo, Australian developers finally have a court ruling mandating real distribution choices on local hardware.
