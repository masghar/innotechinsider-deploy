# Apple’s $5.7B Nightmare: Taction Demands Royalties Through 2035

> After securing a staggering $5.7 billion patent verdict, Taction Technology is demanding an ongoing cut of every iPhone and Apple Watch sold through 2035.

By InnotechInsider Staff · Apple · Oct 19, 2026
Canonical URL: https://innotechinsider.com/apple/apple-taction-patent-verdict-royalty-demands-2035/

> **TL;DR**: Fresh off a record-shattering $5.7 billion patent infringement verdict, Taction Technology is asking a federal judge to impose an ongoing royalty on every iPhone and Apple Watch sold until its haptic patents expire in 2035—putting Cupertino in one of the most perilous legal binds in modern tech history.

Just when Tim Cook’s executive team thought patent litigation could be safely treated as a predictable line-item cost, the bill arrived—with compound interest.

Following a jaw-dropping $5.7 billion jury verdict handed down against Apple in federal court, plaintiff Taction Technology has filed aggressive post-trial motions that could permanently alter the economics of Apple’s most lucrative hardware lines. Taction isn’t merely demanding immediate payment of the multi-billion-dollar lump sum for historical infringement; it wants a court-mandated tollbooth attached to Apple’s supply chain. In a newly unsealed motion for ongoing royalties, Taction is asking the judge to award an ongoing per-unit cut on all infringing Apple devices sold from now until its patent portfolio enters the public domain in late 2035.

If granted, the demand would siphon hundreds of millions of dollars annually from Apple’s balance sheet for the next nine years. The legal showdown is no longer just another squabble in the Eastern District of Texas or the District of Delaware—it has evolved into a high-stakes stress test of how hardware giants license core tactile silicon.

## The Anatomy of a $5.7 Billion Shakedown

The scale of the original judgment rattled corporate counsel across Silicon Valley. While billion-dollar verdicts aren't completely unheard of in tech, they routinely get whittled down or tossed on appeal. But the $5.7 billion awarded to Taction—centered on patents governing advanced electromagnetic actuators and transient vibration damping—represents one of the largest single intellectual property awards in American judicial history.

Taction’s argument at trial was brutally straightforward: Apple’s acclaimed Taptic Engine, the component responsible for the precise, mechanical-feeling clicks beneath the glass screens of modern iPhones and the subtle taps of the Apple Watch, directly incorporates proprietary architectures Taction demonstrated to Apple engineers under non-disclosure agreements over a decade ago.

The jury agreed, finding willful infringement across multiple generations of devices spanning from the iPhone 7 era all the way to this fall’s flagship lineup. Where Apple saw standard engineering iterations of linear resonant actuators (LRAs), the jury saw stolen IP that transformed the user interface of the world’s most profitable consumer electronics franchise.

Because the jury determined Apple’s actions were willful, the presiding judge has the discretion to treble the damages—a theoretical maximum that could push Apple’s immediate liability past $15 billion, though legal analysts consider full trebling unlikely. What is far more terrifying for Cupertino is Taction’s opening bid for future revenues.

## The 2035 Tollbooth: What Taction Is Actually Demanding

In patent law, winning a past damages verdict does not automatically resolve what happens to products rolling off the assembly line tomorrow. Typically, non-practicing entities and research firms seek an injunction to halt sales—a nuclear option severely constrained ever since the Supreme Court's landmark ruling in [eBay Inc. v. MercExchange, L.L.C.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/EBay_Inc._v._MercExchange,_L.L.C.), which made hardware injunctions nearly impossible to secure for licensing-oriented plaintiffs.

Knowing an outright import ban on iPhones and Apple Watches is a non-starter, Taction took the far more lucrative route: the compulsory ongoing royalty.

Taction's filing presents the court with a simple framework. Rather than forcing Apple into an immediate redesign that could cripple consumer markets, the court should levy a running royalty on every device utilizing the contested haptic algorithms and structural damping designs. Taction argues that because willful infringement has already been established by a jury of peers, the post-verdict licensing rate should actually be *higher* than the rate calculated for past damages.

| Proposed Metric | Apple’s Legal Position | Taction’s Motion |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Historical Damages** | Remittitur to under $150M or full retrial | $5.7B + interest + statutory enhancements |
| **Post-Verdict Status** | Full stay pending appellate review | Ongoing compulsory royalty through 2035 |
| **Royalty Base** | Component-level value (fraction of the Taptic Engine) | Entire market value rule applied to end devices |
| **Est. Per-Unit Cut** | $0.10 – $0.25 per device | $1.75 – $2.50 per iPhone; $1.20 per Watch |
| **Estimated 10-Year Exposure** | Under $500M | $3.5B to $5.2B in additional royalties |

At roughly 220 million iPhones and 40 million Apple Watches shipped worldwide annually, an ongoing levy of nearly two dollars per unit would transform Taction into a multi-billion-dollar perpetual revenue engine. For Apple, whose hardware margins are already subject to scrutiny as component costs scale across emerging [apple](/apple/) ecosystem lines, paying a multi-dollar surcharge on every unit sold through 2035 represents an unacceptable precedent.

## Inside the Tech: Why the Taptic Engine Is Vulnerable

To understand why Apple finds itself backed into this legal corner, one has to look at the physics of modern smartphone feedback. Early smartphones relied on clumsy eccentric rotating mass (ERM) motors—essentially tiny spinning counterweights that vibrated the entire chassis indiscriminately. They were slow to start, slow to stop, and utterly incapable of mimicking subtle mechanical sensations.

Taction’s patents cover the mechanical and electrical choreography required to achieve ultra-fast transient responses: accelerating a linear mass violently and then applying an active braking waveform to halt it instantly without resonant "ringing."

Conventional Vibration (ERM):
Slow spin-up -> Loose, muddy oscillation -> Gradual spin-down

Taction Patented Waveform:
Instant peak drive -> Sharp tactile strike -> Active reverse-phase brake (Zero ringing)

Apple mastered this experience with its custom Taptic Engine, using the technology to simulate the click of a physical trackpad, the snap of a camera shutter, and the tactile feedback of virtual iOS dials. Taction proved to the jury that this instant damping effect relied squarely on claims articulated in its patent portfolio, filed in the early 2010s before Apple transitioned away from classic vibrating motors.

Because the patent claims read not merely on the isolated magnetic coil, but on the integrated interaction between the device's main processor, motion-controller firmware, and physical chassis, Taction has successfully argued that the value extends to the full device experience—justifying royalties that transcend the bare manufacturing cost of the actuator itself.

## The Appellate Playbook: Why Tim Cook Won't Cut a Check Yet

Do not expect Apple to quietly wire $5.7 billion or sign an ongoing royalty agreement anytime soon. The playbook for this caliber of patent war is well-established, and the corporate strategy within modern [biz it](/biz-it/) infrastructure relies heavily on institutional stamina.

Apple’s immediate counter-offensive rests on three distinct pillars:

### 1. Motion for Judgment as a Matter of Law (JMOL)
Apple has already filed motions requesting the district judge overturn the jury’s verdict entirely or grant a new trial on damages. Apple argues that the $5.7 billion sum is unmoored from standard patent valuation metrics, asserting that Taction’s experts improperly applied the "entire market value rule" to devices whose appeal relies overwhelmingly on displays, cameras, operating systems, and processors—not haptics.

### 2. The CAFC Circuit Breaker
If the district court upholds the jury's verdict and grants Taction's ongoing royalty, Apple will appeal directly to the [U.S. Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit](https://www.cafc.uscourts.gov/). The CAFC has a well-earned reputation for being skeptical of gargantuan patent awards produced by jury trials. Apple has repeatedly used the Federal Circuit to gut or significantly trim massive damages awards won by litigious competitors, including previous headline-grabbing multi-billion-dollar judgments won by VirnetX and Optis Wireless.

### 3. Patent Trial and Appeal Board (PTAB) Invalidation
Behind the scenes, Apple is bombarding the [U.S. Patent and Trademark Office](https://www.uspto.gov/) with Inter Partes Review (IPR) petitions, seeking to invalidate Taction’s key patent claims on the grounds of prior art. If the PTAB finds the underlying claims obvious or anticipated by earlier academic and industrial papers on vibrational dynamics, the entire legal scaffolding beneath the $5.7 billion award evaporates.

## The Long War Ahead

The terrifying reality for Apple, however, is that this strategy takes years. During that protracted appellate window, the district court could order Apple to pay post-judgment interest—which, on a $5.7 billion sum, accumulates at tens of millions of dollars each month—or force Cupertino to post an astronomical supersedeas bond just to stay execution of the judgment while the appeal crawls forward.

Worse, Taction's bid for post-verdict royalties through 2035 exposes a broader strategic risk. If Apple is forced to redesign its haptic subassemblies to engineer around Taction’s claims, it risks degrading one of the subtle, premium hardware touches that differentiates iOS hardware from commodity Android handsets.

For now, the district court must decide whether to grant Taction’s request for a permanent toll on the world’s most lucrative supply chain. If the judge grants the motion for ongoing royalties, Apple won’t just be fighting to erase a bad week in court—it will be fighting to keep a nine-year, multi-billion-dollar tax off its flagship hardware.
