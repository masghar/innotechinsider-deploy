# Biotech Founder Sam Rusk Wins UW-Madison 2026 Early Career Honors

> UW-Madison honors Envisagenics co-founder Sam Rusk with its 2026 Early Career Award, spotlighting how machine learning for RNA splicing moved into clinical reality.

By InnotechInsider Staff · Science · Nov 2, 2026

> **TL;DR** Envisagenics co-founder Sam Rusk has received the University of Wisconsin-Madison’s 2026 Early Career Award, marking academic validation for software-first drug discovery targeting complex RNA splicing errors.

The University of Wisconsin-Madison College of Engineering has named biotech entrepreneur Sam Rusk as a recipient of its 2026 Early Career Award. Rusk, who co-founded predictive oncology firm Envisagenics after completing her biomedical engineering training at Madison, represents a cohort of founders who bridged the divide between benchtop biochemistry and high-dimensional data infrastructure.

The award honors alumni who have made exceptional industry contributions within their first two decades post-graduation. While engineering faculties have traditionally reserved early-career accolades for hardware innovators and classical biomechanics specialists, Rusk's selection underscores how central software architectures have become to molecular therapeutics.

## Decoding the Transcriptome via Algorithms

Rusk built her career tackling one of the most notoriously chaotic domains in human genomics: alternative RNA splicing. While traditional small molecules target mutated proteins, discovering compounds that regulate aberrant splicing requires mapping how pre-mRNA transcripts are pieced together inside diseased cells. According to research documented by the [National Center for Biotechnology Information](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK21475/), errors in alternative splicing drive thousands of human disease phenotypes, particularly across rare monogenic disorders and treatment-resistant solid tumors.

Envisagenics' proprietary platform, SpliceCore, uses predictive models trained on proprietary RNA sequencing datasets to identify splicing errors that standard transcriptomic tools routinely miss. By combining custom wet-lab assay validation with deep algorithmic triage, the company transformed what was once a multi-year screening bottleneck into a targeted computational query. As venture-backed teams deploy [ai apps](/ai-apps/) to identify previously druggable RNA motifs, academic institutions are paying close attention to which foundational methodologies yield actual clinical candidates rather than empty patent portfolios.

| **Screening Velocity** | Dozens of candidates per quarter | Hundreds of millions of simulated variants weekly |
| **Validation Bottleneck** | Physical sample acquisition | Wet-lab isoform confirmation assays |

## From Madison Labs to Clinical Validation

Rusk’s trajectory illustrates the changing relationship between regional academic hubs and coastal biopharma clusters. After graduating from the [University of Wisconsin-Madison](https://engineering.wisc.edu) Department of Biomedical Engineering, she paired with computational biologist Maria Luisa Pineda to build Envisagenics through Cold Spring Harbor Laboratory's incubator ecosystem.

The technical problem they addressed was commercial scale. Early transcriptomic models produced high rates of false positives when analyzing non-canonical splice junctions, leading pharmaceutical partners down dead-end validation paths. Rusk directed the platform’s business and applied engineering operations toward high-conviction indications, eventually securing development pacts with multinational pharmaceutical developers tracking rare breast cancer mutations and neurodegenerative markers.

The institutional recognition arrives as federal agencies, including the [U.S. Food and Drug Administration](https://www.fda.gov), refine their qualification frameworks for computational tools in drug discovery. As specialized [science](/science/) laboratories incorporate deep learning into fundamental pathology workflows, engineering graduates who can speak the vocabulary of both cellular biology and vector databases have become the industry's most valuable operators.

UW-Madison’s formal acknowledgment of Rusk’s track record highlights an overdue recalibration. The engineering alumni who matter most in 2026 are not merely building medical devices or synthetic scaffolds; they are building the algorithmic foundations that map the baseline code of human disease.
