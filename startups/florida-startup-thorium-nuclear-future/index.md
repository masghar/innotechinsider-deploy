# Florida’s Halcyon Nuclear Banks $140M to Put Thorium Back on the Grid

> St. Petersburg-based Halcyon Nuclear secured fresh venture capital to commercialize molten-salt thorium reactors, bypassing critical bottlenecks in uranium supply.

By Daniel Ashford · Startups · Jun 9, 2026
Canonical URL: https://innotechinsider.com/startups/florida-startup-thorium-nuclear-future/

> **TL;DR** St. Petersburg startup Halcyon Nuclear has closed a $140 million Series B round to build a pilot liquid-salt thorium reactor, offering a hedge against severe uranium enrichment shortages while racing to power energy-hungry regional infrastructure.

The race to supply around-the-clock clean power has pulled almost every flavor of advanced fission out of academic archives and into venture portfolios. Yet as light-water small modular reactor (SMR) developers stumble over supply crunches and rising capital costs, one Gulf Coast startup is taking an alternate chemical route that was sidelined half a century ago.

Halcyon Nuclear, founded in St. Petersburg in 2023, announced this week that it has secured $140 million in Series B financing. Led by Lowercarbon Capital with participation from deep-tech syndicates and industrial off-takers, the capital will fund the construction of a non-nuclear, high-temperature molten salt test facility on Florida’s Space Coast. The company's goal is straightforward: build a factory-fabricated, 100-megawatt thermal molten salt reactor fueled not by scarce enriched uranium, but by abundant thorium.

## Beyond the Uranium Bottleneck

Advanced reactor startups have spent three years running into an uncomfortable geopolitical reality: High-Assay Low-Enriched Uranium (HALEU). Most Generation IV reactor designs require uranium enriched up to 20 percent, compared to the roughly 5 percent used in existing commercial fleets. Because commercial-scale Western enrichment capacity remains slow to spin up, several prominent SMR timelines have slipped toward the end of the decade.

Thorium skirts that specific vulnerability. The silvery metal is roughly three to four times more abundant in the Earth’s crust than uranium, frequently turning up as a discarded byproduct in heavy mineral sands and rare-earth mining operations across North America and Australia.

According to data compiled by the [World Nuclear Association](https://world-nuclear.org/information-library/current-and-future-generation/thorium.html), thorium itself is fertile rather than fissile; it cannot sustain a chain reaction on its own. When loaded into a reactor alongside a modest initiator, thorium-232 absorbs a neutron, transmutes into protactinium-233, and swiftly decays into uranium-233, an exceptional nuclear fuel.

For founders attempting to scale in an era of constrained capital, that distinction changes the industrial supply math. By relying on a fuel source that avoids standard commercial enrichment pathways, Halcyon wants to decouple its operational timeline from federal enrichment queues.

## Inside the Liquid Salt Loop: Thorium vs. Uranium

Halcyon’s core design pulls heavily from work done during the 1960s Molten-Salt Reactor Experiment at [Oak Ridge National Laboratory](https://www.ornl.gov/). Instead of packing solid ceramic fuel pellets inside zirconium alloy rods that sit under immense water pressure, Halcyon dissolves its fuel directly into a molten fluoride salt mixture.

The reactor operates near atmospheric pressure. If a pipe ruptures or pumps fail, the coolant does not flash to steam. Instead, as temperatures rise, the liquid salt expands, naturally pushing the fuel atoms further apart and slowing down the nuclear chain reaction. In a catastrophic scenario, an active freeze-plug melts, draining the liquid fuel into underground, passively cooled containment tanks where it solidifies without human intervention.

| Metric / Parameter | Conventional Light Water (LWR) | HALEU Solid-Fuel SMR | Halcyon Thorium MSR |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Coolant Pressure** | 150+ atmospheres (High) | 1–70 atmospheres (Varies) | ~1 atmosphere (Ambient) |
| **Core Operating Temp** | ~300°C | 500°C – 750°C | 650°C – 700°C |
| **Primary Fuel Source** | Enriched U-235 (3–5%) | Enriched U-235 (15–19.75%) | Thorium-232 / U-233 cycle |
| **High-Level Waste Lifespan** | Tens of thousands of years | Thousands of years | Hundreds of years |
| **Proliferation Footprint** | Spent fuel contains Pu-239 | Varies by design | U-232 contaminants impede weapons diversion |

This high operating temperature yields another distinct economic advantage. Rather than simply boiling water to run steam turbines at 33 percent thermodynamic efficiency, the 700°C output can feed directly into industrial heat processes, hydrogen production, or efficient supercritical carbon-dioxide power cycles. As tech companies hunt for clean capacity in our  reporting, industrial off-takers are increasingly looking at high-heat cogeneration rather than pure electricity generation.

## The Florida Factor: Space Coast Talent and Grid Pressures

Florida might seem an unusual venue for a nuclear startup compared to traditional atomic hubs like Idaho Falls or Oak Ridge. Yet Halcyon’s leadership points to two regional mechanics driving their choice of headquarters: specialized manufacturing talent and extreme grid strain.

With the aerospace corridor between Orlando and Melbourne humming, Florida possesses an enviable density of high-temperature metallurgy technicians, precision turbomachinery specialists, and automated valve fabricators. These trades are precisely what advanced fluid-loop reactors demand. Halcyon has already recruited heavily from former propulsion engineers who spent years qualifying nickel-based superalloys for rocket engines.

At the same time, the Southeast is running up against serious electricity bottlenecks. Between rapid population migration and an influx of large-scale infrastructure projects—tracked heavily across our  coverage—utilities throughout the region are seeking zero-carbon generation that doesn't demand vast tracts of land. A 100-megawatt thermal footprint takes up a fraction of the acreage required by utility-scale solar arrays paired with battery installations, making it an attractive proposition for localized commercial campuses.

## Regulatory Traps and Commercial Realities

Despite the technological elegance of liquid-salt architectures, Halcyon must navigate substantial hurdles that have tripped up advanced reactor ventures for decades.

The first is chemistry. Molten fluoride salts, while chemically stable at elevated temperatures, are notoriously corrosive to common structural metals when exposed to trace impurities or neutron flux. Halcyon is betting on modern Hastelloy derivatives and silicon carbide composites that were unavailable to 20th-century metallurgists, but proving these materials can survive a decade of continuous hot operation remains unproven at commercial scale.

The second is regulatory licensing. The U.S. Nuclear Regulatory Commission was built around water-cooled, solid-fuel reactors. While the agency has spent recent years crafting the risk-informed [NRC Part 53](https://www.nrc.gov/reactors/new-reactors/advanced/rulemaking-and-guidance/part-53.html) licensing pathway for non-light-water designs, clearing non-LWR licensing remains a multi-year, capital-intensive grind. Handling uranium-233 also involves tracking trace uranium-232, whose decay chain emits hard gamma radiation. While this makes the fuel virtually impossible to divert for weapons without detection, it demands entirely automated, remotely shielded fuel-handling equipment on the plant floor.

Halcyon is bypassing the licensing queue for now by running its Series B hardware trials entirely with non-radioactive surrogate salts in Brevard County. If those high-pressure fluid dynamics and heat exchangers perform as modeled through late 2027, the startup intends to submit its formal construction permit application for an operational prototype. Thorium has spent decades as clean energy's most compelling theoretical standby; Halcyon's newly flush balance sheet will test whether that theory can finally withstand the heat of commercial deployment.
