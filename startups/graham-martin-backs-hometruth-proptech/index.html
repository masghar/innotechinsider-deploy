Graham Martin Backs HomeTruth to Solve Real Estate’s Due Diligence Crisis

HomeTruth names serial entrepreneur Graham Martin Chairman after securing fresh capital, targeting real estate’s costly, opaque transaction failure problem.

IS
InnotechInsider Staff

Oct 15, 2026, 10:35 AM UTC · 8 min read

TL;DR Proptech upstart HomeTruth has secured backing from veteran entrepreneur Graham Martin, appointing him Chairman as the startup deploys automated property intelligence to dismantle residential real estate’s costliest failure point: blind transactions.

Residential real estate has spent the better part of a decade digitizing storefronts while leaving the plumbing untouched. We have algorithmic mortgage calculators, hyper-slick spatial listing videos, and automated valuation models that churn out automated appraisal estimates in milliseconds. Yet, when an ordinary buyer actually enters the transaction pipeline, the entire process grinds into a bureaucratic halt that feels closer to the nineteenth century than late 2026.

Across major global markets, more than a quarter of residential property purchases collapse between offer acceptance and contract exchange. The culprit is rarely sudden buyer remorse; it is structural opacity. Hidden restrictive covenants, unpermitted structural work, historical subsidence issues, and outdated title claims routinely ambush buyers weeks into the process, torpedoing transactions after thousands of dollars or pounds have been sunk on surveys and legal retainers.

HomeTruth, a property intelligence startup launched to eliminate transaction fall-throughs by front-loading due diligence, announced today that seasoned tech entrepreneur and investor Graham Martin has joined its board as Chairman and lead angel investor in its latest expansion round.

The appointment signals a deliberate strategic transition for HomeTruth. The startup is shifting from a promising regional data aggregator into an aggressive enterprise infrastructure provider, aiming to fundamentally rewrite the legal disclosure workflow for property transactions.

modern commercial real estate architectural model blueprint desk — Photo by Zack Yeo on Unsplash

The High Cost of Transaction Blind Spots

To understand why Martin is placing capital behind HomeTruth, one has to examine the systemic failure rate of residential conveyancing. The mechanics of buying a home in jurisdictions relying on the doctrine of caveat emptor (“buyer beware”) place nearly all due diligence liability on the purchaser, as detailed in the legal framework of conveyancing. Traditionally, that investigation only begins after an offer is accepted.

For two to four months, legal teams exchange fragmented questions across disconnected email chains, requesting physical documentation from regional authorities and scouring local registries. When HM Land Registry or municipal databases take weeks to confirm whether an extension complies with local planning ordinances or if a drainage easement cuts across a rear garden, chains freeze. If a severe defect is discovered late, the chain snaps.

HomeTruth’s platform inverts this pipeline. Instead of post-offer discovery, the company aggregates local authority planning filings, geospatial flood and climate risk models, spatial environmental registry updates, digital land titles, and building regulation sign-offs into a single, cryptographically verified “Property Passport” before a listing goes live.

By tying automated ingestion engines to public registries, HomeTruth generates an exhaustive due diligence audit within minutes, highlighting red-flag encumbrances and structural disputes before an agent even books a viewing. When enterprises deploy modern biz it software architectures to handle legacy paper workflows, transaction velocity increases exponentially—a dynamic HomeTruth is actively exploiting to compress transaction timelines from months to days.

Enter Graham Martin: From Venture Builder to Boardroom Anchor

Graham Martin’s arrival brings institutional discipline to a company that, until now, has operated primarily as an agile data engineering shop. Martin, whose track record spans multiple exits across enterprise software, telecommunications, and digital transformation plays over three decades, is known for scaling regulatory-heavy B2B platforms.

Martin isn’t treating HomeTruth as a passive advisory post. He has taken a direct equity stake in the venture and will take hands-on leadership of board governance, strategic mergers and acquisitions, and international enterprise partnerships.

“The residential property market remains one of the last bastions of asymmetric information,” Martin noted regarding his appointment. “Listing portals spent fifteen years optimizing how homes are marketed, but virtually nobody spent time fixing how homes are verified. HomeTruth has built the core data plumbing to turn real estate into a high-trust, low-friction asset class. My role is to accelerate our integration with institutional lenders, national agency networks, and the legal sector.”

Martin’s appointment follows a period where speculative proptech—characterized by capital-intensive iBuying programs that attempted to flip houses via purely financial arbitrage—has largely fallen out of favor with venture syndicates. Institutional capital in late 2026 demands capital-efficient enterprise infrastructure that generates recurring software revenue while solving genuine structural pain points.

The Technology: Multi-Modal Ingestion Over Surface-Level AI

HomeTruth’s core defensibility does not lie in a generic conversational wrapper slapped onto a large language model. Instead, it relies on a bespoke multi-modal ingestion pipeline designed to digest unstructured, legacy municipal archives that date back decades.

Local government planning registries are notorious for their lack of standardization. Approvals are frequently buried in scanned microfiche documents, hand-drawn boundary modifications, and inconsistently indexed PDF attachments. HomeTruth’s underlying data engine uses specialized optical character recognition paired with spatial semantic models to map historical deeds against live cadastral maps.

When the system parses a restrictive covenant drafted in 1954 preventing secondary outbuildings or commercial vehicle parking, it translates archaic legal terminology into plain-English risk assessments for agents, conveyancers, and buyers. Simultaneously, it cross-references the parcel against public datasets maintained by consumer protection agencies like the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau and regional environmental monitors to evaluate predatory loan history or local flood-plain re-zoning.

By pairing these audit capabilities with hardened data security controls, HomeTruth ensures that sensitive property deeds, financial records, and proprietary legal surveys remain tamper-proof and fully compliant with evolving regional privacy mandates.

The Battle for the Agent’s Desktop

HomeTruth’s most significant operational hurdle is not technology; it is adoption. Real estate agents are notoriously platform-fatigued, bombarded weekly by point solutions claiming to streamline their workflow. Furthermore, conveyancers and real estate attorneys often operate on billable hours, creating a structural disincentive to compress due diligence timelines.

To bypass this institutional inertia, Martin and the HomeTruth executive team are targeting their distribution via enterprise APIs rather than standalone software dashboards. By embedding their data layer directly into CRM giants and modern legal practice management tools, HomeTruth runs quietly in the background:

  1. Pre-Listing Automated Scrub: The moment an agent enters a parcel identifier into their listing system, HomeTruth pulls all publicly available deeds, planning permits, and environmental datasets.
  2. Flag Resolution Protocol: If unpermitted alterations or missing compliance certificates are detected, the system notifies the vendor to resolve the paperwork before buyers view the property.
  3. Consolidated Seller Pack: When a buyer makes an offer, their lender and legal representative are issued an authenticated audit pack, eliminating redundant search queries.

Regulatory Tailwinds and the Future of Upfront Information

The broader macro environment is swinging heavily in HomeTruth’s favor. Regulatory bodies across North America, the UK, and Europe are aggressively pushing for mandatory “material information” disclosures at the point of marketing. Regulators have made it clear that marketing a property without disclosing critical constraints—such as cladding liabilities, conservation status, or flood risks—leaves brokerages vulnerable to severe consumer protection sanctions.

Independent brokerages and enterprise franchises that previously resisted upfront documentation now find themselves legally compelled to provide it. For HomeTruth, this regulatory shift transforms their software from a discretionary luxury into mandatory compliance infrastructure.

“We are moving out of the era where proptech meant shiny consumer search portals,” said Martin. “The next generation of value will be captured by companies operating as invisible utilities—the foundational data layers that de-risk major capital allocations. If a consumer cannot buy a five-hundred-thousand-dollar home with the same transparency and speed as purchasing a used car on a smartphone, the system is fundamentally broken. We intend to fix it.”

What Comes Next

With Graham Martin occupying the Chairman’s seat, HomeTruth is reportedly planning to accelerate strategic enterprise hiring and initiate formal integration partnerships with mortgage underwriters.

The ambition is significant: to transition mortgage underwriting from speculative forward-looking estimates to instant, data-backed approvals predicated on verified structural and legal property profiles. If HomeTruth succeeds, the concept of a deal collapsing three months in because an attorney discovered an unpermitted conservatory will soon become an archaic relic of real estate’s analog past. Martin’s appointment demonstrates that serious capital is ready to wager that real estate’s long-delayed reckoning with data transparency has finally arrived.
