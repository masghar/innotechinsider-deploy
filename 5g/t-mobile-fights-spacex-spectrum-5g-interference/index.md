# T-Mobile Fights SpaceX Over Spectrum Rules and 5G Interference

> T-Mobile has urged the FCC to reject SpaceX’s push for relaxed spectrum rules, warning satellite downlinks could degrade urban mobile broadband networks.

By InnotechInsider Staff · 5G & Connectivity · Oct 22, 2026
Canonical URL: https://innotechinsider.com/5g/t-mobile-fights-spacex-spectrum-5g-interference/

> **TL;DR** T-Mobile has formally pushed back against SpaceX's regulatory requests at the FCC, arguing that Starlink's direct-to-cell expansion cannot come at the expense of terrestrial cellular signal integrity.

The commercial alliance between T-Mobile and SpaceX is hitting the limits of radio physics. In filings before the [Federal Communications Commission](https://www.fcc.gov/), T-Mobile warned that SpaceX's efforts to secure broader operating waivers and looser out-of-band emissions limits could severely impair terrestrial mobile networks across the United States.

The move highlights an escalating conflict over orbital-to-ground signals. While both companies have spent two years collaborating on satellite-to-cellular messaging services, SpaceX’s broader ambitions to unlock higher-throughput voice and broadband over mobile frequencies have put it on a collision course with the very carrier that helped legitimize its direct-to-cell strategy.

## The Physics of Orbital Clutter

SpaceX's second-generation direct-to-cell Starlink constellation relies on massive phased-array antennas to beam RF energy directly to standard smartphones. To deliver higher throughput without exhausting narrow licensed bands, SpaceX petitioned regulators to ease aggregate equivalent power flux-density (EPFD) limits and permit more flexible use of adjacent mobile-satellite service (MSS) frequencies.

T-Mobile contends that these technical allowances would cross a dangerous line. In its latest technical filings, the carrier pointed out that relaxing emission floors creates aggregate noise floors high enough to desensitize base station receivers on the ground. When orbital transmissions leak excess energy into neighboring downlink and uplink bands, base stations in dense urban corridors must ramp up error correction, reducing total cell capacity and degrading speeds for conventional subscribers.

The dispute has forced traditional telecom rivals into the same corner. AT&T and Verizon filed parallel objections, but T-Mobile's dissent carries unique weight because the carrier already maintains a direct spectrum-sharing agreement with SpaceX in the PCS G-block (1910–1915 MHz and 1990–1995 MHz). For businesses balancing capital expenditure against hybrid mobile systems covered under [biz it](/biz-it/) strategies, regulatory stability around licensed spectrum remains a primary requirement.

| Filing Parameter | SpaceX Petition Request | Carrier Counter-Position (T-Mobile / AT&T) |
|---|---|---|
| **Out-of-band emissions (OOBE)** | Relax aggregate limits for MSS downlinks | Strictly enforce existing Part 25 interference baselines |
| **Power Flux-Density (EPFD)** | Permit localized spikes during low-elevation passes | Cap orbital signal strength to prevent base-station receiver desensitization |
| **Inter-operator Coordination** | Real-time dynamic spectrum management via software | Rigid hard frequency guards without software-based override |
| **Operational Scope** | Nationwide coverage inclusive of urban/suburban zones | Restriction to rural "white spaces" devoid of terrestrial 5G nodes |

## Partners in Public, Opponents in Dockets

The regulatory friction reveals an operational contradiction. On consumer stages, both companies promote a seamless safety net for off-grid travelers, using the orbiters to close rural dead zones. Yet behind closed doors at regulatory agencies, the long-term roadmap for [spacex](/spacex/) depends on acquiring substantial access to sub-3 GHz frequencies to monetize voice and high-speed data downlinks.

Engineering teams at T-Mobile argue that satellites should serve as a coverage gap filler, not a parallel infrastructure layer that degrades baseline city performance. Ground-level carrier operations depend on strict adherence to [3GPP radio standards](https://www.3gpp.org/) to maintain dense small-cell deployments. If low Earth orbit constellations push higher out-of-band radiation without rigid attenuation filters, the carriers argue that urban macro-cells will suffer elevated noise interference, hurting everyday users who never leave metropolitan coverage.

SpaceX has defended its position by arguing that conservative regulatory limits established decades ago were designed for geostationary orbiters, not modern, fast-moving low-Earth constellations capable of dynamic beam steering. The company maintains that software-defined interference mitigation can suppress signal bleed before it impacts terrestrial towers. Carriers, however, remain unconvinced that proprietary satellite tracking algorithms provide sufficient defense against physical [spurious emissions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spurious_emission).

## A Crucial Test for the FCC

The commission now faces a delicate balancing act. Allowing satellite operators to push RF power limits speeds up broadband redundancy across underserved regions, but doing so risks eroding network performance for hundreds of millions of urban smartphone users.

As regulators review technical studies from both sides, the clash proves that orbital direct-to-device services cannot simply bypass the realities of ground infrastructure. For the rollout of hybrid [5g](/5g/) constellations, the ultimate gatekeeper is not orbital launch capacity, but the strict physics of radio spectrum co-existence. The FCC's pending ruling will establish how much noise terrestrial networks must tolerate from above—and whether satellite ambitions have finally reached their terrestrial ceiling.
