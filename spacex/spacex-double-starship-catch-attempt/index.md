# SpaceX Targets First Double Starship Catch on Upcoming Test Flight

> SpaceX plans to catch both Super Heavy and the Starship spacecraft during an upcoming flight. The feat relies on Starbase's dual-tower launch infrastructure.

By InnotechInsider Staff · SpaceX & Space · Oct 23, 2026
Canonical URL: https://innotechinsider.com/spacex/spacex-double-starship-catch-attempt/

> **TL;DR** SpaceX is preparing to catch both the Super Heavy booster and the Starship upper stage at Starbase during an upcoming orbital test, marking the most ambitious recovery test in the program's history.

Two years after demonstrating that a 232-foot rocket booster could be plucked out of the sky by a pair of mechanical chopsticks, SpaceX is doubling down. Elon Musk confirmed this week that the company will attempt to catch both stages of the vehicle—the Super Heavy first stage and the Starship spacecraft itself—during the program's next integrated flight test.

The test will push Starbase's expanded ground infrastructure to its operational limits. While the initial booster catch in October 2024 required only a single launch tower, capturing both halves of the world's most powerful launch system requires the coordination of Starbase’s newly completed twin-tower setup.

## The Dual-Tower Gambit

Catching Super Heavy roughly seven minutes after launch has become a proven procedure for SpaceX’s Boca Chica team. Catching the ship, however, is a different challenge altogether. Because Starship enters orbit (or an orbital-speed trajectory), it cannot simply turn around and fly back immediately. Instead, the upper stage must complete a planned lap around Earth, survive atmospheric reentry, and steer toward the coastal South Texas launch site with pinpoint accuracy.

To pull this off without landing a second vehicle on top of the first, SpaceX is splitting the recovery work between Launch Complexes Pad A and Pad B. The company’s ongoing filings with the [Federal Aviation Administration](https://www.faa.gov/space) reflect safety zone modifications consistent with dual-site recoveries. As aerospace engineers develop new iterations in [future tech](/future-tech/) manufacturing, ground infrastructure has evolved from passive concrete pads into dynamic catching machines.

|---|---|---|
| **Entry Velocity** | ~Mach 3 to 4 (suborbital boostback) | ~Mach 25 (orbital reentry) |
| **Thermal Protection** | Stainless steel with hot-staging ring | Hexagonal ceramic heat shield tiles |

| **Target Infrastructure** | Starbase Tower A | Starbase Tower B |

## Taming the Upper Stage Return

Catching the ship is mechanically far riskier than catching the booster. When Super Heavy returns, its propellant tanks are nearly empty, but its structural steel skin has endured only brief aerodynamic heating. The upper stage, by contrast, must slam through the upper atmosphere at 17,500 miles per hour, heating its underbelly to temperatures exceeding 1,400 degrees Celsius.

![SpaceX Starship upper stage thermal protection tiles](https://images.unsplash.com/photo-1541185933-ef5d8ed016c2?ixid=M3w5MDk5NzB8MHwxfHNlYXJjaHwxfHxTcGFjZVglMjBTdGFyc2hpcCUyMHVwcGVyJTIwc3RhZ2UlMjB0aGVybWFsJTIwcHJvdGVjdGlvbiUyMHRpbGVzfGVufDB8MHx8fDE3OTI3NTg2NTF8MA&ixlib=rb-4.1.0 "SpaceX Starship upper stage thermal protection tiles — Photo by SpaceX on Unsplash")

Thermal protection tile loss has dogged previous flights. If tiles fail near the ship’s hard points—the load-bearing pins designed to slot into the tower's mechanical arms—the structure could warp under aerodynamic load, making a safe capture impossible. The vehicle must kill its orbital velocity, transition from a horizontal belly-flop into a vertical orientation using its center Raptor engines, and decelerate to a dead hover between the chopstick arms.

Musk acknowledged that early attempts to catch the ship carry a high probability of failure. The upper stage may be diverted to an ocean splashdown if telemetry reveals telemetry drift, thruster anomalies, or structural damage during reentry. As commercial teams push [science](/science/) instrumentation further into harsh thermal environments, every square foot of tile data gathered during descent guides whether Mission Control gives the "go" for land return.

## Rapid Turnaround Demands Full Reuse

The technical push comes down to economics. SpaceX’s contracts for [NASA Artemis](https://www.nasa.gov/humans-in-space/artemis/) missions require rapid launches to fill orbital propellant depots before an uncrewed Starship can head for the Moon. Throwing away the upper stage on every launch balloons costs and throttles launch cadence to the rate at which Starfactory can build new spacecraft hulls.

Catching both stages on the same flight eliminates marine recovery operations entirely, allowing SpaceX to roll both elements directly back to refurbishment bays. If the recovery succeeds, it proves the core thesis behind the Starship architecture: an orbital space vehicle that operates more like a twin-engine commercial airliner than a traditional expendable missile. If it fails, the company will have to rebuild its newest launch tower—a tradeoff SpaceX has shown it is entirely willing to make.
