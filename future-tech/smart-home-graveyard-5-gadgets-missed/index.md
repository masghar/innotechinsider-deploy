# The Smart Home's Graveyard: 5 Gadgets We Still Miss

> The smart home promised seamless living, but its early days are littered with brilliant ideas and broken promises. We dive into the digital graveyard to mourn five innovative gadgets that died too soon, leaving users adrift.

> **TL;DR** The smart home frontier is a brutal landscape where innovation often clashes with market realities, leaving a trail of brilliant but discontinued gadgets. We mourn five such devices – from the pioneering Revolv Hub to Apple's original HomePod – exploring what their demise teaches us about platform risk, vendor commitment, and the true cost of convenience in connected living.

The smart home, for all its futuristic allure, has a surprisingly robust graveyard. It’s a boneyard populated by promising devices, ambitious platforms, and the occasional outright betrayal. For every Alexa or HomeKit success story, there are a dozen fascinating failures, gadgets that either couldn't find their footing, were cannibalized by corporate giants, or simply ahead of their time. These aren't just defunct products; they're cautionary tales, stark reminders that the cutting edge often dulls quickly, and consumer trust is a fragile commodity in the ever-shifting sands of connected tech.

As a seasoned observer of this volatile industry, I’ve seen countless innovations rise and fall. But some exits sting more than others. They represent not just a financial loss for early adopters, but the fading of a particular vision, a unique approach to making our homes smarter. This isn't just nostalgia; it's a critical examination of how the smart home, despite its rapid growth, still struggles with fundamental issues of longevity, interoperability, and vendor commitment. Let's dig up five such devices that, despite their demise, still hold a special place in the hearts of tech enthusiasts and offer invaluable lessons for the future.

## The Smart Home's Shifting Sands: A Landscape of Promise and Peril

The smart home dream is simple: a dwelling that anticipates our needs, responds to our commands, and seamlessly integrates technology into our daily lives. Yet, the reality has been far more complex. From competing standards and fragmented ecosystems to security vulnerabilities and the dreaded subscription creep, the journey towards a truly intelligent home has been fraught with peril. It's a high-stakes game where even well-funded ventures can crumble, often leaving users with expensive, bricked hardware. This inherent instability makes every purchase a gamble, a bet on a company's longevity and commitment.

The devices we're about to revisit weren't just quirky experiments; many were pioneers, attempting to solve real problems or offer genuinely new experiences. Their failures, or rather, their engineered obsolescence, highlight deeper issues within the smart home industry – issues that continue to plague us today.

## Revolv Hub: The Original Betrayal

If there's one device that epitomizes the smart home's potential for heartbreak, it's the **Revolv Hub**. Launched in 2013, Revolv was one of the early, ambitious attempts at creating a universal smart home controller. It supported multiple wireless protocols – Z-Wave, Zigbee, Insteon, Wi-Fi, Bluetooth – aiming to be the central brain for all your disparate smart gadgets. Its promise was elegant simplicity: one app, one hub, unified control. For early adopters, it was a revelation, a glimpse into a truly integrated smart home future.

Then came Google. In 2014, Nest (then part of Google) acquired Revolv. The acquisition itself wasn't surprising, as Nest was aggressively expanding its smart home footprint. What followed, however, was unprecedented and set a dangerous precedent for the industry. In 2016, Nest announced it was shutting down Revolv's cloud services, effectively bricking every single Revolv Hub in existence. No more updates, no more functionality – just a useless piece of plastic.

This wasn't a product that faded away due to lack of interest; it was deliberately killed by its new corporate owner. The outcry was immense, and rightly so. It exposed the brutal reality of "platform risk" in the smart home: when you buy into a cloud-dependent ecosystem, you're entirely at the mercy of the vendor's whims. Revolv users felt utterly betrayed, and the incident cast a long shadow over consumer trust in connected devices, highlighting the need for transparent end-of-life policies or, better yet, open standards that prevent such wholesale obsolescence. ## Nest Secure: Google's Security Graveyard

Google makes this list twice, and for good reason. Its track record with smart home product longevity has been, to put it mildly, inconsistent. The **Nest Secure** alarm system, launched in 2017, was a prime example of Google's ambition in the security space. It featured a sleek, modern design, integrating a central hub (Nest Guard) with sensors (Nest Detect) and a key fob (Nest Tag). It promised a simple, elegant security solution, deeply integrated with the Nest ecosystem of cameras and thermostats. For many, it represented a compelling alternative to traditional, clunky alarm systems.

Fast forward to 2020: Google announced it was discontinuing Nest Secure, ceasing sales of new units. While existing systems would continue to function for a time, the writing was on the wall. Then, in April 2024, Google delivered the final blow: the Nest Secure service would officially shut down, rendering all systems inoperative. Google offered users a free professional monitoring system from ADT (with a required contract) or a credit towards a Google Store purchase.

The discontinuation of a *security* system is particularly egregious. Unlike a smart light bulb, a security system is meant to provide peace of mind and protection. For Google to pull the plug, forcing users to migrate or face an unprotected home, is a significant breach of trust. It raises serious questions about Google's commitment to its hardware products and the long-term viability of proprietary smart home security solutions. What happens to critical infrastructure like your home's security when a tech giant decides to pivot? It's a chilling thought for [data security](/data-security).

## Apple HomePod (Original): A Premium Silence

Apple’s venture into the smart speaker market with the original **HomePod**, launched in 2018, was typical Apple: uncompromising design, premium audio quality, and a hefty price tag. Unlike competitors like Amazon Echo or Google Home, which prioritized smart assistant functionality and affordability, the HomePod’s primary focus was on delivering an unparalleled audio experience, positioning it as a high-fidelity speaker first, and a smart assistant second. Its computational audio capabilities were genuinely impressive, filling rooms with rich, balanced sound.

However, its limitations were equally clear. Siri, while improving, lagged behind Alexa and Google Assistant in terms of versatility and knowledge domain. Its closed ecosystem meant limited streaming service support outside of Apple Music, and its smart home control was restricted to HomeKit-compatible devices. At its initial price of $349 (later reduced to $299), it was a tough sell for many, especially when more capable assistants were available for a fraction of the cost.

In March 2021, Apple announced it was discontinuing the original HomePod to focus on the more affordable and popular HomePod mini. While the mini is a competent speaker, it doesn't quite match the acoustic prowess of its larger sibling. Many audio enthusiasts and Apple devotees still lament the loss of the original HomePod, seeing it as a missed opportunity for Apple to establish a truly premium audio experience in the smart home space. It represented a bold, albeit flawed, vision for a smart speaker that prioritized sound above all else, a vision that, for now, remains unfulfilled by [apple](/apple).

## Essential Home: The Dream That Never Was

Andy Rubin, the co-founder of Android, launched Essential Products with much fanfare in 2017. While the Essential Phone grabbed most of the headlines, the company also unveiled the **Essential Home**, an ambitious smart home hub designed to be the central nervous system of your digital dwelling. What made Essential Home so intriguing was its philosophy: a commitment to open platforms, privacy, and a custom operating system called Ambient OS, which promised to be proactive and context-aware.

The Essential Home was envisioned as a beautiful, circular device with a large touchscreen, designed to blend seamlessly into modern decor. It aimed to learn your routines, anticipate your needs, and manage your smart home devices without constantly needing voice commands. It was meant to be a privacy-first device, processing as much data locally as possible. The concept was visionary, aiming to solve many of the fragmentation and privacy concerns that plague the smart home today.

Unfortunately, the Essential Home never saw the light of day. Essential Products struggled financially, and after the Essential Phone failed to gain significant traction, the company announced its shutdown in 2020. With it, the promise of the Essential Home and Ambient OS vanished. It’s a classic tale of a brilliant concept, backed by a visionary, that couldn't overcome the harsh realities of the startup world. For those who believed in a more intelligent, private, and open smart home, the Essential Home remains a potent symbol of what could have been. [startups](/startups)

## Wink Hub: The Slow, Painful Demise

Unlike the abrupt execution of Revolv or the strategic discontinuation of the HomePod, the **Wink Hub** has experienced a prolonged, agonizing decline. Launched in 2014 by Quirky (a company known for crowdsourcing inventions), Wink aimed to be another universal smart home hub, supporting Z-Wave, Zigbee, Lutron Clear Connect, Kidde, and Bluetooth. It gained popularity for its user-friendly app and broad compatibility, quickly becoming a favorite for those wanting to connect devices from various brands under one roof.

Wink's journey, however, has been a rollercoaster of corporate acquisitions and financial instability. After Quirky filed for bankruptcy, Wink was acquired by Flextronics, then by i.am+ (will.i.am's tech company), and eventually by its current owner, a subsidiary of a private equity firm. Each transition brought uncertainty, but the biggest blow came in 2020 when Wink announced it was moving to a mandatory monthly subscription model. Without paying the fee, users would lose control of their devices.

This move sparked outrage and alienation among its user base, many of whom had invested heavily in the ecosystem under the premise of free basic functionality. While Wink still technically exists, its service has become notoriously unreliable, with frequent outages, slow performance, and a general lack of development or support. It's a zombie hub, technically alive but offering a shell of its former self. The Wink story is a grim reminder that even established platforms can be crippled by mismanagement and a short-sighted focus on monetization over user experience. It underscores the importance of choosing a smart home ecosystem backed by a stable, committed, and transparent company. [^1^](#ref1)

## Lessons from the Digital Graveyard

The demise of these five gadgets, and countless others, offers crucial insights for both consumers and manufacturers navigating the smart home landscape.

For consumers, the primary takeaway is **caution against vendor lock-in and platform risk**. Cloud-dependent devices are inherently vulnerable to corporate decisions. Prioritizing open standards (like Matter, a promising new standard [^2^](#ref2)) and devices that can function locally, or at least with multiple platforms, can offer a degree of future-proofing. Always consider a company's financial stability and long-term commitment before investing heavily in their ecosystem. A well-researched purchase today can save you from a bricked device tomorrow.

For manufacturers, these failures highlight the paramount importance of **long-term commitment and consumer trust**. Killing off a product, especially a security system or a foundational hub, erodes confidence in the entire industry. Clear communication, generous migration paths, and a genuine understanding of the impact on users should be standard practice. The smart home's full potential will only be realized when users can invest in solutions with confidence, knowing their devices will remain functional and supported for a reasonable lifespan, not just until the next corporate pivot.

## The Future of Connected Living: Beyond the Graveyard

The smart home market continues to evolve at a blistering pace. New technologies like AI at the edge, enhanced privacy features, and genuine interoperability standards are emerging. The lessons learned from these "dead" gadgets are invaluable as we move forward. The industry must prioritize open ecosystems, local control, and a commitment to longevity over proprietary walled gardens and planned obsolescence.

The smart home of tomorrow needs to be resilient, adaptable, and, most importantly, trustworthy. We miss these five gadgets not just for their innovative spirit, but for the questions their premature exits force us to ask about the very nature of our connected lives. Their legacy is a call to action for a smarter, more sustainable, and ultimately, more reliable smart home future.
